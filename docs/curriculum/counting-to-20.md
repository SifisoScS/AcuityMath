## 1. Two item shapes, and what distinguishes them

| | picture-choice | numeral-choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The child sees | a group, and three more groups | a group, and three numbers |
| Picks | `"the first one"` … | `"14"` … |
| `verification.measure` | `count` | `numeral` |
| Can carry `count-by-spread` | yes | no — nothing is drawn to misjudge |
| Can carry `teen-digits-reversed` | no — a position cannot express *41* | yes |
| In this batch | 11 | 11 |

**`problem_type` does not tell you which shape an item is.** The corpus
spreads all four types evenly across both — `foundations-count-to-5` has
five `match-set` items and `foundations-match-numeral-to-5` has five
`count-set` ones. The field is the pedagogical tag; `verification.measure`
is the shape. This batch follows that, because departing from it would make
`counting-to-20` the one concept where the tag means something else.

## 2. How a quantity is drawn

Six to ten is one row. **Eleven upwards is a full row of ten and a
remainder** — `figures` becomes `[10, 4]` under `layout: "stack"`.

What that buys is narrower than it first looks, and the first draft
overstated it. A bare row of ten is **not** seen as ten; nothing is drawn
around it, so a child has to count it like anything else. What the split
does buy is real but smaller: the remainder is left subitisable — four is
four at a glance in a way fourteen never is — and the ten becomes a chunk
that recurs identically across every teen item, which is the thing a child
can eventually learn to take on trust. Ten-frame *recognition* would need a
frame drawn round it, and the schema has no way to ask for one.

> It is also the reason F0e happened. The gate read `figures[0].count` and
> would have rejected every teen item here as *"the picture draws 10 but the
> problem is about 14"*. Chasing that found twelve `foundations` problems
> the same rule had already condemned and F0b had already corrupted. The
> rule now sums every group, and this batch is the first content authored
> against the corrected one.

**One frame across a problem's three candidate pictures**, sized to the
largest — the corpus does this in all 160 problems that have candidate
pictures, without exception. The reason is `count-by-spread` itself: a card
sized to its own contents makes the widest card visibly different, and the
child can answer a question about *how many* without counting.

**Candidates never mix one-row and two-row drawings.** No corpus problem
does — 0 of 160 — and the reason is the same one: a card with a different
silhouette can be picked out, or ruled out, without being counted. Given
the rule above, this has a consequence worth stating outright: **ten and
eleven can never be candidates on the same table**, because ten draws as
one row and eleven as two.

The bracketed number is which of the three positions carries that error.
Difficulty is 4 and cognitive load 2 throughout; §6 says why they are flat.

**The candidates are never drawn in the prompt's shape.** The first draft
used one shape for everything, which made the correct card a pixel-for-pixel
copy of the prompt — a child could match the two images and never count
anything. `foundations-count-to-5` switches shape in all twenty of its
items, and it is switching for exactly this reason.

**`count-by-spread` is computed, not asserted.** The card carrying it holds
*fewer* shapes than the answer and is spaced so that it is **the widest
drawing on the table** — wider than the correct card and wider than the
other distractor. The first draft only made it as wide as the correct card,
which left the genuinely widest card sitting under `off-by-one-overcount`
and put the rationale on the wrong option.

It appears only on the single-digit items. Once both pictures open with a
full row of ten, width stops being something they differ in, and the
rationale would be a label rather than a fact.

### 4.1 `teen-digits-reversed`, and where it does not apply

The new error type: *four-teen* says the four first, so a child writing or
choosing what they hear reaches for `41`.

**The mechanism needs the name to lead with the unit digit, and most teen
names do not.** The first draft offered the reversal for 12, 13 and 15 as
well, and that was wrong:

| number | name | leads with the unit? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 11 | *eleven* | no — no unit is audible at all |
| 12 | *twelve* | no — nothing in it says *two* |
| 13 | *thirteen* | eroded — *thir-*, not *three* |
| 15 | *fifteen* | eroded — *fif-*, not *five* |
| 14, 16, 17, 18, 19 | *four-*, *six-*, *seven-*, *eigh-*, *nine-* | yes |

So the reversal is offered on **14, 16, 17 and 18** only. Eleven and twelve
give a child no reason whatever to reach for 11 or 21; thirteen and fifteen
are arguable and are left out rather than argued for. Nineteen would qualify
and is a picture item instead. Twenty reverses to `02`, which nobody writes.

This is the difference between an error type and a label. `21` sitting under
a picture of twelve is not a teen reversal — it is just a wrong number, and
calling it a reversal would have taught the remediation loop to drill a
confusion the child never had.

## 6. What is provisional

`teen-digits-reversed` **has not been reviewed by anyone who teaches
six-year-olds**. §4.1 narrows it to the four numbers whose names produce
the mechanism, which makes it a sharper claim than the draft's and still
an untested one. The same caveat covers the other eighteen new error types
in `age-7-bridge.md` §8.

**The difficulty and load numbers are flat on purpose.** The draft had four
values — teens harder than single digits, numerals harder than pictures —
and two things were wrong with that. It put the single-digit picture items
at difficulty 3, which is `foundations-count-to-5`'s own number, so the
batch asserted that counting to nine is exactly as hard as counting to
five. And the teen/single-digit gap was invented: nobody measured it.

The corpus's own practice is one value per concept per measure —
`foundations-count-to-5` is 3/2 across all twenty of its problems and
`foundations-match-numeral-to-5` is 4/3 across all twenty of its. So this
batch carries two numbers, each one step above its prerequisite: **4/2 for
the picture items, 5/3 for the numeral ones.** That asserts exactly one
thing — this concept is harder than the two it is built on — and leaves the
rest to the 3PL parameters, which are learned from attempts rather than
guessed here.

Counting nineteen things really is harder than counting seven. That
difference is real and is deliberately not encoded, because no one has
measured how much harder and a number typed today would be indistinguishable
from one that had been.

## 7. What the review changed

Five defects, none of which any gate can see. They are recorded because the
batch verified 22 of 22 against the gate **before** they were fixed, which
is the entire argument for reading the tables.

| # | defect | why no gate catches it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Prompt and candidates drawn in one shape, so the correct card was a copy of the prompt and could be matched without counting | the gate checks counts, and every count was right |
| 2 | `count-by-spread` sat on a card that was not the widest; the widest was the off-by-one card | a rationale is a string; nothing compares it to the picture |
| 3 | One item mixed a one-row candidate with two-row candidates, making it eliminable by silhouette | group counts were internally consistent |
| 4 | `teen-digits-reversed` claimed for 12, 13 and 15, whose names do not produce it | the distractor is a valid numeral and not the answer |
| 5 | Difficulty 3 on the single-digit items — the prerequisite's own number — and an invented teen/single-digit split | no check reads difficulty at all |

Defects 1, 2 and 3 are about **drawings that are correct and still wrong**:
every count agreed with every target, and the pictures still handed the
answer away. Defect 4 is an **invented error type**, which is the failure
mode `age-7-bridge.md` §8 warns about, caught in its first use. Defect 5 is
a **formulaic number**, which is what §6 now refuses to produce.

## 8. What the review could not fix

**Nothing in the application draws these pictures.** The `visual` object is
served to the client and handed to `MathManipulatives`, which reads
`visualData.initialCount`, `.itemType`, `.shapeType` and `.formula` — none
of which exist in this schema — and which only draws at all when
`visualType` is set to one of five names. `InfiniteAdaptiveModal` hardcodes
`visualType: undefined` for every served problem, so every branch is skipped
and the default card renders, which prints the prompt text back under the
heading *Math Expression*.

For a picture-choice item that means the child is shown a sentence, the same
sentence again in monospace, and three buttons reading *the first one*, *the
middle one*, *the last one*, with nothing drawn to tell them apart. The item
is not hard; it is unanswerable, and guessing is the only available strategy.

**This is not new and not this batch's doing.** All 180 `foundations`
problems are in the same state and have been since they were imported. But
it bears directly on what reviewing a picture is worth: every drawing
decision in §2 and §3 — the ten-and-remainder split, the shape switch, the
spread card, the shared silhouette — is currently invisible. They are
correct against the schema and against the corpus's conventions, and no
child has ever seen one.

A renderer for this schema is a piece of work in its own right and belongs
in the roadmap rather than in an authoring pass.
